\subsection{Modeling Contamination in RAG-Based QA}
We quantify leakage along the two channels formalized in \S\ref{sec:methods-contam}: \emph{pretraining} (matches to $D_M$) and \emph{retrieval-time} (matches in $\mathcal{R}(q)$). For each item $x{=}(q,a)$, we query web search with the raw question string only (we never concatenate the answer) to form $\mathcal{R}(q)$, and apply span-based, auditable detectors to two views, question-only $\pi_{\text{in}}$ and question+answer $\pi_{\text{in+lab}}$, recording a positive if any detector fires (Eq.~\eqref{eq:unified}) and, where required, the answer-bearing variant (Eq.~\eqref{eq:answer-bearing}). For pretraining, we approximate full-membership tests by retrieving a candidate pool from C4 with BM25 (default $k{=}1000$) and evaluating the same criteria over that pool.

\paragraph{Dataset-level rates.}
Figure~\ref{fig:contamination_rates_by_dataset} summarizes per-dataset prevalence under both channels and both views. Several consistent patterns emerge. First, question-only detectors flag more often than question+answer detectors across channels, reflecting that near-duplicates or paraphrases of the question are more common than full question+answer co-occurrences. In the pretraining channel, the highest question-only rates are observed for \textsc{SimpleQA} (11.5\%) and \textsc{FreshQA} (9.6\%), followed by \textsc{HotpotQA} (9.4\%), with lower rates for \textsc{WebQuestions} (7.7\%) and \textsc{SQuAD} (3.9\%); the corresponding question+answer rates are smaller (4.2\%, 4.2\%, 5.0\%, 3.6\%, and 2.6\%, respectively). In the retrieval-time channel, \textsc{SQuAD} shows the largest exposure (11.0\% question-only; 9.2\% question+answer), followed by \textsc{HotpotQA} (7.2\%; 5.8\%), \textsc{SimpleQA} (5.4\%; 4.6\%), \textsc{WebQuestions} (2.6\%; 1.5\%), and \textsc{FreshQA} (2.0\%; 1.8\%). Because our procedure uses span-based audits and finite candidate pools, these figures are conservative and should be read as lower bounds.

\paragraph{Where contamination comes from.}
Figure~\ref{fig:top_contamination_sources_by_dataset} breaks down the top retrieval sources per dataset. Wikipedia is a common contributor across datasets; \textsc{SQuAD} additionally surfaces dataset-hosting pages (e.g., \texttt{rajpurkar.github.io}) alongside Wikipedia; \textsc{HotpotQA} often surfaces model or paper aggregation sites (e.g., \texttt{huggingface.co}, arXiv, ACL Anthology); \textsc{SimpleQA} likewise shows \texttt{huggingface.co} and Wikipedia. These sources illustrate that retrieval-time leakage can arrive via encyclopedic sites as well as pages that mirror or discuss benchmark content.

\paragraph{How early it appears in search.}
Figure~\ref{fig:rank_distribution_collage} shows rank distributions for the contaminated pages surfaced during retrieval. Across all datasets, answer-bearing contamination (question+answer view) appears substantially earlier in the list than question-only matches. Mean contaminated ranks (question-only vs.\ question+answer) are $23.7$ vs.\ $7.4$ for \textsc{WebQuestions}, $16.3$ vs.\ $7.9$ for \textsc{SQuAD}, $15.8$ vs.\ $9.7$ for \textsc{HotpotQA}, $15.0$ vs.\ $2.8$ for \textsc{FreshQA}, and $19.9$ vs.\ $8.1$ for \textsc{SimpleQA}. Thus, when answer-bearing material is retrievable, it typically occurs within the first page of results, increasing the risk that a Web-RAG system can rely on lookup.

\paragraph{Curated evaluation splits.}
For downstream analyses we construct two 500-example splits per the protocol in \S\ref{sec:methods-contam}: a \emph{Highly Contaminated (HC)} split and a \emph{Randomly Sampled (RS)} split. Both splits include an equal number of items from each dataset (100 per dataset; 5 datasets; 500 total), and are stratified so that both contamination channels are represented within each dataset. The aggregate compositions are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:contamination_nested_donut_combined}. In the HC split, most items are flagged in at least one channel (pretraining: 52.2\%, retrieval-time: 32.4\%, both: 5.6\%, none: 9.8\%). In the RS split, the majority show no detected leakage (none: 88.8\%), with small fractions flagged for retrieval-time (4.0\%), pretraining (6.4\%), or both (0.8\%). These two splits let us separately study performance where leakage is likely versus typical random sampling, and we use them throughout the subsequent subsections.

(Table~\ref{tab:omnibus}). With 10{,}000 permutations the $p$-value resolution is

negligible variance explained by ``round''.

Table \ref{tab:dataset-view-3view-colored} summarizes performance by model and seed split. For each variant of the seed item -- Original, Paraphrase (mean R1-R10), and Dynamic (mean R1-R10) -- we report Base and Web-RAG accuracies, the change relative to the Original within the split (Para-Orig, Dyn-Orig), and the Web gain $\Delta$ defined as Web-Base.


Across models, three patterns are apparent. (i) Paraphrases are typically associated with modest reductions under Base and larger reductions under Web-RAG. (ii) Dynamic variants generally show penalties that are comparable to or smaller than paraphrases, while retaining more of the Web gain. (iii) On the highly contaminated split, the Web gain for Original items is often larger than for paraphrase or dynamic variants; paraphrasing reduces this gain more strongly on that split.

These observations are consistent with a view in which surface form changes mainly affect retrieval pathways, whereas dynamic rewrites alter the question while preserving topic and evidence regime in a way that remains useful to retrieval. All comparisons are made within split; we do not draw conclusions from between-split mean differences.
